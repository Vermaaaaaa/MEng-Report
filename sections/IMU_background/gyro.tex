\subsection{Gyroscope: Operations and Errors}
The gyroscope is the main component that is used to determine the orientation 
of the object. It measures the angular rate in its orthogonal axes $\omega^x,\omega^y,\omega^z$
which is used to determine the object's orientation at a discrete time. The orientation is 
determined through the integration of angular rate.
In this chapter, the quaternion representation is selected due to 
being more computationally efficient than other methods \cite{zhang_2011_a} and does
not suffer from gimbal lock \cite{yang_2014_body}. Gimbal lock is described as a phenomenon where two of the three rotational axes of an object align causing a loss of one degree of freedom (DOF), since
the Euler angle representation becomes singular.


\subsubsection{Angular Rate Integration}
\label{sec:gyroint}
We can define a quaternion that is represented by the 
angular rate, where $\omega$ is the real angular rate, and $\omega_q(t)$ is the pure quaternion. 
Note: Both the attitude and updated quaternions should be normalised to ensure quaternions represent rotations.
$q_k \leftarrow \frac{q_k}{\|q_k\|}$.

\begin{equation}
\omega_q(t) = [0,\ \omega_x(t),\ \omega_y(t),\ \omega_z(t)]
\end{equation}

The orientation evolves according to \cite{yang_2014_body}

\begin{equation}
\hat{q}_k = \hat{q}_{k-1} \otimes \Delta q_k
\end{equation}

where $\Delta q_k$ refers to the SO3 exponential map, which maps rotational vectors to incremental rotation quaternions \cite{yang_2014_body}.

\begin{equation}
\Delta q_k = \begin{bmatrix}
    \frac{\omega}{|\omega|} \sin{\frac{|\omega| \Delta t}{2}} \\
    \cos{\frac{|\omega| \Delta t}{2}}
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}


This was all done by using an ideal $\omega_k$, if we were to model the angular
rate from a gyroscope as \cite{trawny_2005_indirect}

\begin{equation}
\omega_{m,k} = \omega_k + b_k + n_k 
\end{equation}

where $\omega_{m,k}$ is the measured angular rate, $\omega_k$ is the true angular rate,
$b_k$ is the bias term, and $n_k$ is the noise term. The real quaternion orientation
update is as follows where $\Delta q_k$ becomes $\Delta q_{m,k}$.

\begin{equation}
\Delta q_{m,k} = \begin{bmatrix}
    \frac{\omega_{m,k}}{|\omega_{m,k}|} \sin{\frac{|\omega_{m,k}| \Delta t}{2}} \\
    \cos{\frac{|\omega_{m,k}| \Delta t}{2}}
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

and the update is now

\begin{equation}
\hat{q}_k = \hat{q}_{k-1} \otimes \Delta q_{m,k}
\end{equation}

This shows that the inclusion of the bias and noise accumulates over samples
as we move forward to the next k, $q_{k-1}$ will incorporate the previous error
terms. This results in an accumulated orientation error.

For more information about this derivation see Chapter 12\cite{yang_2014_body}.






\subsubsection{Gyroscope Error Sources}
\label{sec:gyroerrors}
\subsubsection*{Constant Bias}
The bias of a gyroscope is the average output from the gyroscope when it is not undergoing any
rotation \cite{woodman_2007_number}. This is measured in $ \degree /h$ and can be estimated by taking an average
of the output. As this bias is constant, the drift it causes grows linearly with time. 
However, as discussed further in this section other error sources can make this
difficult to determine. Figure 1 shows how constant bias changes the output.

\subsubsection*{White Noise / Angle Random Walk (ARW)}
The gyroscope is also affected by some white noise \cite{woodman_2007_number}. This white noise sequence is zero-mean uncorrelated
random variables between samples and across axes. When the gyroscope signal is integrated to obtain
an angle, this white noise produces an ARW.
The units of ARW are denoted by $ \degree / \sqrt{h}$, this shows that the deviation of angle error grows proportionally to $\sqrt{t}$.
As ARW is due to random variables, it is classified as the $1\sigma$ of the orientation error. Analog Devices
shows this in figure 2, where the ARW is $ 0.17\degree / \sqrt{h}$.
